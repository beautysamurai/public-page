**Summary:** This empirical study uses approximately 588 million Polymarket trades from 2\.48 million wallets to examine the favorite–longshot bias \(FLB\), variation in returns across aggregation units, and persistence of demand at the price tails\. The authors report an aggregate FLB but argue that its magnitude and sign depend on whether observations are aggregated by child market, invested dollars, or parent event\. This review is based only on the abstract and Introduction of the 51\-page paper; the body, proofs, experiments, and appendices were not supplied or checked\.

**Main result:** According to the authors, purchases below 10 cents lose 19\.35 cents per dollar on a dollar\-weighted basis, while purchases at or above 90 cents earn 0\.83 cents\. Longshots lose 6\.30% under equal child\-market weighting but reverse to a 4\.09% gain under equal parent\-event weighting\. The authors also report a two\-sided FLB in Crypto and Politics but not in Sports\. Wallets with the strongest past longshot preference reportedly account for 26\.6% of next\-month low\-price purchases yet earn returns similar to those of other wallets\. These are author claims stated in the abstract and Introduction; the underlying estimates, robustness checks, proofs, and appendices were not examined\.

**Practical application:** Execution analysis and risk monitoring in prediction markets should not interpret average losses on low\-price tokens as a uniform pricing bias without decomposing results by the number of child markets, parent event, traded dollars, and category\. Trader segmentation should also avoid treating recurrent longshot purchases alone as a proxy for concentrated losses\.

**Methodology:** The study uses version 1\.3 of the Polymarket Users dataset, covering November 2022 through March 2026, and compares purchase prices in resolved markets with final payoffs\. For longshots and favorites, it compares equal child\-market weighting, pooling by invested dollars, and equal parent\-event weighting after pooling within each event\. Wallet price\-tail preferences are classified using only prior purchases, after which subsequent purchase shares and returns are examined\. The authors also state that they analyze differences across categories, experience, order methods, reported fees, and collateral\-sharing conditions\.

**Limitations:** The full 51\-page paper was not provided; only the abstract and Introduction were available\. Consequently, the sample construction, return definitions, standard errors, confidence intervals, missing\-data and selection biases, wallet classification, robustness checks, proofs, experiments, and appendices could not be examined\. Wallets do not map one\-to\-one to individuals because one person may control multiple wallets\. The observational design does not identify a causal explanation, and the findings are highly sensitive to parent\-event construction and aggregation weights\. All reported figures should therefore be treated as author claims\.

**Why read it:** The paper is highly relevant to prediction\-market microstructure and execution assessment because it argues, using a very large transaction dataset, that measured FLB depends strongly on the aggregation choice\. However, this is a limited\-source review and cannot assess reliability or reproducibility, so the recommendation is conditional\.
